You're Allowed to Be Nervous. Here's What That Actually Looks Like at a Boudoir Session.

Almost every client who books a boudoir session says some version of the same thing before they arrive: I'm excited, but I'm also really nervous.

That's not unusual. That's the norm.

I've never had a client walk in completely at ease, without a single second thought. What I have had, over and over, is clients who walk in nervous and walk out saying they had no idea it would feel the way it did.

Here's what that actually looks like.

The nervousness usually peaks in the parking lot.

Most people feel most anxious right before they arrive. The drive over, the moment before they knock. Once they're inside, something shifts. The studio is quiet. It's private. There's no audience, no judgment, no one rushing through. The first ten minutes are mostly just settling in — looking through wardrobe, talking through the session, having a coffee if you want one. By the time we start shooting, the energy in the room is usually completely different from what you walked in with.

You don't need to know what you're doing.

This is probably the most common fear: I don't know how to pose, I don't know what to do with my hands, I'm going to look awkward. You won't, because every single thing is guided. Where to put your hands, where to look, how to angle your body, when to hold still. I do this for a living. My job is to make you look incredible — and I can't do that if you're left guessing. You'll never be standing in front of the camera wondering what to do next.

Bringing someone helps more than people expect.

If the idea of walking in alone makes the nerves worse, bring someone with you. A partner, a friend, a sister. Someone who makes you laugh and makes you feel like yourself. They're there from the moment you arrive. A lot of clients say afterward that having someone familiar in the room made all the difference — not because anything inappropriate happened, but because having that person there meant they could relax in a way they might not have otherwise.

The moment it clicks.

There's usually a moment about fifteen or twenty minutes in where something shifts. You see a preview on the camera screen, or you just stop thinking and start feeling — and you realise you're doing it. That moment is different for every person, but I've seen it enough times to know it almost always comes.

What nervousness looks like in the photos.

Nothing. The nervousness doesn't show. It never does. What shows is you — in a space where someone is paying careful attention to your light, your angles, your expression, specifically to show you at your most beautiful. That's what the photos are made of.
